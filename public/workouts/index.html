Workout Plans and Weekly Splits Built for Real Schedules

Pick a weekly training split that matches your equipment and available days, then see how to adjust the week when work, travel, or a crowded gym interrupts a session.

By IZEM Editorial · Accountable publisher: Mohammed Jebbari · Updated October 13, 2026

Quick decision rule: Choose a split based on the number of days you can consistently complete in a busy week. Two or three days calls for full-body sessions; four days fits an upper/lower rotation; five days suits an upper/lower plus push-pull-legs hybrid. Hitting each major muscle group at least twice per week supports hypertrophy across all three setups [1].

Educational fitness planning for healthy adults. Stop any exercise that causes sharp pain, dizziness, or joint instability, and consult a qualified physician or physical therapist before training around an injury or medical condition.

Compare 2-day to 5-day training splits

A program fails when a five-day template gets squeezed into a three-day reality. When you skip the back half of a body-part split, certain muscle groups go ten days without training. The table below matches weekly availability to a split structure that keeps muscle frequency balanced even if one workout shifts.

Days per week Recommended split Default schedule Weekly sets per muscle Best fallback when you miss 1 day
2 days Full Body A / B Tue · Sat (or Mon · Thu) 6 to 10 hard sets Shift the missed day by 24 to 48 hours; keep at least one rest day between sessions.
3 days Full Body A / B / C Mon · Wed · Fri 9 to 14 hard sets Merge the main hinge or press from Day 2 into Day 3 and drop two isolation sets.
4 days Upper / Lower (2x each) Mon · Tue · Thu · Fri 10 to 16 hard sets Convert the final two sessions into one Full Body priority session so every muscle still gets trained twice.
5 days Upper / Lower + Push / Pull / Legs Mon · Tue · Thu · Fri · Sat 12 to 18 hard sets Drop the fifth day into a 4-day Upper/Lower week; never stack four heavy days in a row.

How many exercises and sets belong in each workout?

Adding extra exercises rarely rescues an inconsistent week. Controlled trials and meta-analyses on resistance training volume show a dose-response relationship up to roughly 10 to 20 weekly sets per muscle group, with diminishing returns when a single session piles on junk volume [2]. Most 40-minute sessions need four to six movements:

  1. Primary lower-body or upper-body compound lift (3 to 4 sets of 6 to 10 reps): Squat, hinge, horizontal press, or overhead press performed while coordination is fresh.
  2. Antagonist or complementary compound lift (3 sets of 8 to 12 reps): Pair a push with a row or pulldown, or pair a knee-dominant lift with a hip hinge.
  3. Unilateral or secondary multi-joint movement (2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps): Split squats, lunges, incline dumbbell presses, or single-arm rows to address side-to-side balance.
  4. Targeted isolation and trunk work (2 sets of 10 to 15 reps): Lateral raises, curls, triceps extensions, or loaded carries that fit your remaining time window.

Frequently asked questions

Which workout split is best for 3 days a week?

A 3-day full-body split (such as Monday, Wednesday, and Friday) works well for most lifters training three times per week because each major muscle group is trained at least twice weekly while leaving four recovery days [1].

What should I do if I miss one workout in a 3-day or 4-day split?

Keep the rest of the week intact instead of cramming two full workouts into one day. Move the primary compound lifts from the missed session into your next scheduled workout, drop one or two lower-priority isolation sets if time is tight, and resume your normal calendar next Monday.

How many sets per muscle group per week do I need?

Meta-analyses on resistance training volume indicate that 10 to 20 challenging weekly sets per muscle group support hypertrophy for many intermediates, while beginners and busy lifters can still progress on 6 to 10 hard sets per muscle group per week [2] [3].
