Use the Coach-or-Tracker Test

This is the link-worthy test I would use before paying for any AI fitness subscription. Give an app one point for each question it can answer clearly. A score is not a verdict by itself; it shows what kind of product you are buying.

  1. Does it reach out before the decision? A coach can create a useful cue before the usual skip point. A tracker waits to be opened.
  2. Does it review what actually happened? The app should capture why the workout or meal plan broke, not only whether a box was checked.
  3. Can it produce one realistic fallback? A 45-minute plan should become a useful 15-minute version without turning into a random list of exercises.
  4. Do workouts and nutrition share context? The plan should not treat training, meals, recovery, and schedule as unrelated tabs.
  5. Can equipment reality change the session? Available machines, a hotel gym, or a home setup should influence the plan when relevant.
  6. Does it remember recurring friction? You should not have to explain the same late meeting, food preference, or crowded-gym problem every week.
  7. Can it explain the change and its limits? You should understand why the plan changed, correct bad context, and know when human help is the better choice.

How to read the score: 0–2 points usually means a library or tracker. 3–5 points means an adaptive planner. 6–7 points means the product is attempting a complete coaching loop. Verify the experience yourself; a feature page does not prove that every suggestion will be useful.

The competitor angle most comparisons miss

Most “best fitness app” lists compare libraries: number of workouts, exercise videos, recipes, or integrations. Those matter after you open the app. The harder moment often happens before that: it is 5:45 PM, the gym was the plan, and you are already negotiating with yourself.

A pre-workout call changes that moment. The useful version is not a robotic “time to exercise” message. It knows the planned session, asks what changed, and gives one decision: full workout, realistic fallback, or an honest reschedule. The evening review then captures what happened, so the weekly plan can change instead of repeating the same failed Tuesday.

Founder insight: a tracker records the gap between your plan and your life. A coach tries to close it before the day is over.

What the first IZEM week should look like

1. Choose one goal and real workout daysBuild the week around your calendar, experience, food preferences, and available equipment—not an ideal schedule.
2. Answer the coach call and startUse food or equipment scanning when it removes uncertainty, then complete the first useful version of the workout.
3. Review the day and let the plan adaptExplain what worked and what did not. The next week should reflect the pattern instead of punishing a missed box.

When a human trainer is still the better choice

Use a qualified human when you need hands-on technique coaching, injury rehabilitation, medical nutrition therapy, diagnosis, or live professional judgment. An AI app can help with general fitness structure and everyday decisions; it should not pretend to see pain, movement quality, or clinical risk that it cannot assess.
