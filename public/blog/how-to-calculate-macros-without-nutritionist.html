Crack the Code: How to Calculate Your Macros on Your Own

August 6, 2026 · Published by Mohammed Jebbari, IZEM founder · AI-assisted draft

You can calculate your macros without a nutritionist by first finding your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) to determine your target daily calories. From there, allocate your daily calories by dedicating 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight, setting fats at 20% to 30% of total calories, and filling the remaining calories with carbohydrates.

Understanding nutrition does not require an expensive degree or a costly consulting session. Once you learn the basic math behind energy balance, you can customize your daily intake to build muscle, drop body fat, or simply maintain your current weight with confidence.

How do I find my daily calorie starting point?

Before you can split your daily food into protein, carbs, and fats, you need to know how much total energy your body burns. This baseline is known as your Total Daily Energy Expenditure, or TDEE. It represents the sum of your resting metabolism, the energy used to digest food, and all of your physical movement throughout the day.

To estimate this number on your own, you start with your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR). A reliable way to calculate BMR is the Mifflin-St Jeor formula. For men, the math is: 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age (y) + 5. For women, the formula is: 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age (y) - 161.

Once you have your BMR, multiply it by an activity factor to find your final TDEE. Here is how activity levels translate into multipliers:

  • Sedentary (little to no exercise): BMR x 1.2
  • Lightly active (light exercise 1-3 days/week): BMR x 1.375
  • Moderately active (moderate exercise 3-5 days/week): BMR x 1.55
  • Very active (hard exercise 6-7 days/week): BMR x 1.725

Let us look at a hypothetical scenario to see how this works in practice. Suppose a 30-year-old active individual weighs 80 kilograms (176 pounds) and stands 180 centimeters tall. Their estimated BMR would be roughly 1,780 calories. Because they exercise four days a week, we multiply that BMR by 1.55, resulting in a TDEE of approximately 2,759 calories per day.

If this hypothetical person wants to lose fat, they might subtract 500 calories to target a safe, sustainable deficit of 2,259 calories. If their goal is muscle growth, they might add 250 calories to support recovery without excessive fat gain. This basic number serves as the foundational bucket from which all of your individual macronutrients will be drawn.

What is the best macro split for my fitness goals?

Once you have your target daily calories, you need to divide them into the three main macronutrients: protein, fats, and carbohydrates. Protein and carbohydrates contain 4 calories per gram, while dietary fat contains 9 calories per gram. This difference in caloric density is why fats can cause your daily calorie total to climb quickly if you do not keep an eye on them.

Your first and most important priority should be your protein intake. Protein is responsible for preserving lean muscle tissue during weight loss and rebuilding muscle fibers after intense resistance training. A standard, science-supported guideline is to consume between 1.6 and 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight (about 0.8 to 1.0 grams per pound).

After establishing your protein target, you can allocate your daily dietary fats. Fats are essential for hormone production, joint health, and absorbing fat-soluble vitamins. Setting your fats between 20% and 30% of your total daily calories is generally ideal for health and dietary adherence. Any remaining daily calories are then assigned to carbohydrates, which fuel your brain and muscle performance.

Let us return to our hypothetical individual who is targeting 2,259 calories for fat loss. If we set their protein to 2.0 grams per kilogram, they will consume 160 grams of protein daily, which equals 640 calories. If they allocate 25% of their total energy to fats, that is about 565 calories, or roughly 63 grams of fat. The remaining 1,054 calories go to carbohydrates, which equals about 263 grams of carbs. This balanced split keeps performance high while steadily reducing body fat.

How do I adjust my macros when my progress stalls?

No initial calculation is perfect because formulas only provide an educated estimate. Your real-world metabolism is dynamic, reacting to changes in your daily movement, sleep quality, and stress levels. If you follow your calculated macros for three weeks and do not notice any weight change, you must make a calculated adjustment rather than panicking or slashing your calories in half.

If your goal is weight loss and the scale has stopped moving, try reducing your daily intake by 100 to 200 calories. This small adjustment should come primarily from your carbohydrates or dietary fats, leaving your protein intake high to protect your muscle tissue. For example, you might remove 25 grams of carbs or 10 grams of fat while keeping your protein target exactly the same.

Conversely, if you are trying to build muscle but your weight remains flat, increase your daily intake by 150 to 250 calories. Monitor how your body responds over the next fortnight. Small, incremental shifts are far easier to manage and will keep your energy levels steady throughout your training week.

Calculating your baseline numbers is the easy part. The real difficulty lies in the daily friction of looking up nutritional labels, guessing portion sizes at restaurants, and resisting the urge to quit when life gets busy. Many people become overwhelmed by the administrative burden of logging every single gram of food into a blank spreadsheet or a complicated app database.

Traditional tracking also risks fostering an unhealthy mental relationship with nutrition. This rigid approach often leads to burnout, causing people to abandon their fitness goals altogether within the first month.
