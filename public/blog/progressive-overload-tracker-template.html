Direct answer: a useful progressive overload tracker records enough context to answer two questions before the next session: “What did I actually do last time?” and “What am I trying to improve next?” This template keeps each working set visible without pretending one progression rule fits every program.

Scope: this is a logging and planning tool, not a prescription. Your program, exercise technique, recovery, health history, pain, and sport-specific needs can change what a sensible next target looks like. If pain or a health concern is affecting training, use qualified guidance rather than forcing a number upward.

Why this template does not auto-prescribe your next weight

Progressive overload is broader than “add weight every workout.” Depending on the program and goal, progress can involve more load, more repetitions, more sets, better execution, more range of motion, or another planned change. The tracker therefore stores your next target but does not invent one for you.

The American College of Sports Medicine’s 2026 resistance-training update emphasizes consistency and individualization rather than one rigid rule for everyone. That is a useful boundary for any progression tool: the log should make your program easier to follow, not replace program design or professional judgment. Read the ACSM guidance.

Worked example: compare before you change

Previous session: bench press, three working sets at the same load, with the final set losing repetitions.

Useful log: keep all three sets, add a short effort note, and write the next target supplied by your program.

What not to infer automatically: one difficult set does not prove you must add weight, reduce weight, add sets, or deload. Training decisions depend on the program and the wider context.
