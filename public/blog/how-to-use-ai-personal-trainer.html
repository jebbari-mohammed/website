How to use an AI personal trainer

Give your AI personal trainer a clear training brief, then check the plan.

Start with your real schedule, equipment and recent training. After the workout, log what you actually did so the next recommendation has useful information.

Start with your current week

“Get me fit” leaves a lot for an app to assume. A clearer starting point is: “I want to build a consistent strength routine. I have Monday and Friday evenings, 35 minutes each, and adjustable dumbbells at home.” That describes a goal and the limits the plan must respect. The days and minutes are an example, not a recommended training dose.

Use the app's profile settings where they exist. Fitbod's current My Plan documentation, for example, separates goals, equipment, training days, duration and experience. It also lets users specify available weight increments. Those settings affect its recommendations; another app may collect the same information differently. [1]

Write down what you've done recently. “Returning after a long break” tells a coach more than an old “intermediate” label. Include other activity that takes effort, such as sports or long runs, instead of describing only gym sessions. You don't need to attempt a maximum lift to complete this brief.

Be literal about equipment. “Dumbbells” could mean one fixed pair or an adjustable set with several increments. Say which you have and use kilograms or pounds consistently. A plan that needs a cable machine is still unsuitable if your app calls your room a home gym.

If a clinician has given you exercise restrictions, follow that guidance and ask what activity is appropriate. A chatbot's confident reply doesn't clear a medical condition, injury or rehabilitation plan. The American Heart Association's consumer guidance highlights those situations as reasons for professional oversight. [3]

Find the controls you'll use during the workout

The products in search results offer different ways to train. FitnessAI describes sets, reps, weights and exercise swaps; Zing describes workout progression plus audio and video demonstrations. Find the edit, demonstration and logging controls in your chosen app before starting. FitnessAI's product page · Zing's publisher listing.

If you prefer spoken guidance, check whether it guides the session or just reads exercise cues. Ray's comparison makes that distinction. If you're using a plan-writing tool such as Manus, decide where you'll keep the workout and record your sets.

Check access before paying. GymStreak's listing, for example, shows a free download with in-app purchases and describes a subscription for complete access. Confirm the offer, renewal and cancellation terms that apply to your account.

Make a training brief you can keep

Fill in what you know and leave anything else open. The text can help you complete an app's profile or start a coaching conversation. Check what a service collects and shares before pasting it there. Keep medical records and identifying details out of this form.

Read the workout before you start

A formatted plan can still omit the information you need on the gym floor. Check the proposal against your brief, then ask about anything missing. This review catches practical mismatches; passing it doesn't prove that the workload or technique is appropriate for you.

A plan that doesn't match its brief

Suppose someone entered Monday and Friday, 35 minutes each, with adjustable dumbbells and no bench. The app returns four weekly sessions, a barbell squat and a cable row. Those are visible input mismatches before anyone attempts a repetition.

A useful correction is: “Keep Monday and Friday as the only training days. I have adjustable dumbbells and no bench, barbell or cable machine. Rework the exercise choices around that equipment, explain unfamiliar movements and include warm-up and rest in the 35-minute estimate.”

Check the revised version again. This hypothetical example fixes calendar and equipment assumptions; it doesn't establish which exercises, sets or loads that person should do.

Use the first session to make an accurate record

Review the exercise instructions and prepare the equipment before you start. Warm up, use a manageable load and give yourself time to learn the logging controls. Mayo Clinic advises learning proper form and reducing the weight or repetitions if you can't maintain it. Stop an exercise that causes pain. [4]

Record completed repetitions and the weight you actually used, with units. If you changed an exercise, stopped a set or ran out of time, include that. Marking the entire proposal as completed leaves the next recommendation with an inaccurate training record.

Keep the note specific: “Finished the first two exercises; stopped when the 35 minutes ended” is more useful than “bad workout.” You can then ask about the remaining work instead of automatically squeezing it into the next session. For a longer record, use our workout tracking template.

If the app cites a study to justify its advice, open the source and check that it exists and says what the app claims. The American Heart Association's guidance makes the same point: a plausible-looking reference can be wrong. [3]
