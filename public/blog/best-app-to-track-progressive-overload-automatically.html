The IZEM LOAD test for a progressive-overload app

Most “progressive overload” comparisons mix logging, programming, analytics, and coaching into one score. LOAD separates the four decisions that matter when your goal is simply to progress a lift without guessing.

L · Last performance

Can you see what happened?

The app should make the previous weight, reps, sets, or relevant performance easy to retrieve while you train.

O · Objective next target

Does it tell you what to beat?

A progression assistant should make the next target explicit rather than forcing you to infer it from charts.

A · Adjustment rule

What happens after a miss?

Look for a documented response when the target is too hard, training is interrupted, or available equipment changes.

D · Decision clarity

Can you understand the rule?

You should know whether the app is following your program, a fixed progression scheme, or its own adaptive recommendations.

Logger, progression assistant, or adaptive coach?

These categories solve different problems. Paying for the most automated option is wasteful if you already follow a well-defined program; choosing a bare logger is frustrating if you specifically want the app to decide the next target.

What “progressive overload” should not mean

It should not mean increasing weight every workout regardless of performance. The American College of Sports Medicine’s 2026 resistance-training guidance emphasizes individualization, consistency, and tailoring load and volume to the person’s goal; it also notes that complex programming is not automatically better for the average healthy adult. That is a useful check on any app that treats “more weight next time” as the only possible progression decision.

Safety boundary: an app can organize training decisions, but it cannot diagnose pain, injury, or illness from a workout log. If a recommendation conflicts with symptoms, hands-on technique needs, or medical guidance, do not follow the app merely to preserve a progression streak.

A five-minute test before you subscribe

  1. Open one exercise history. Can you see the exact prior working sets without digging through several screens?
  2. Ask what the next target is. Is there an explicit number or rule, or only a graph?
  3. Simulate a missed target. Check the product documentation: does the next recommendation change, or are you expected to edit it manually?
  4. Check the progression unit. Weight is not the only variable. Determine whether the app supports the progression style your program actually uses.
  5. Check the boundary. If the app also sells nutrition, recovery, or coaching, decide whether those extras solve a real problem for you or distract from the lifting job.
